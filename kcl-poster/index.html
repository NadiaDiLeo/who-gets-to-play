Physical and conceptual accessibility in video games

Accessible version of the poster submitted to the Arts & Humanities PGR research poster competition, King’s College London, November 2026.

Video games are a central part of contemporary culture, yet not everyone gets to play them on equal terms. My PhD project studies what keeps players out, both in how games are built and in the culture around them.

Physical accessibility

Can you perceive and operate the game?

A Deaf player needs subtitles; a player with limited hand movement may need to move actions to other buttons. Physical accessibility covers the features that decide whether someone can see, hear and control a game. It also covers cognitive load and difficulty settings.

Both studies use the Game Accessibility Guidelines (GAG): around 120 checkable recommendations written by studios, accessibility specialists and researchers, in three levels:

  • Basic: e.g. subtitles for all speech
  • Intermediate: e.g. remapping every control
  • Advanced: e.g. screen-reader support

Study 1. What disabled players write about

Di Leo & Tarantino, DiGRA 2026

Can I Play That? publishes accessibility reviews by disabled players. We matched 479 of them (2019–2025) against every guideline. Reviewers write mostly about basic features for hearing, movement and sight; advanced ones rarely appear, and twenty guidelines never come up.

Study 2. Testing the award nominees

Di Leo, Games and Culture, in press

The Game Awards gives a prize for Innovation in Accessibility. With a second evaluator, I played the first hour of each 2025 nominee (First-Time User Experience; Barnett et al., 2018) and checked all 121 guidelines. All five did well, but fewer are met as the guidelines get harder:

  • 95.1% Basic guidelines
  • 83.7% Intermediate guidelines
  • 51.8% Advanced guidelines

Blind players are left furthest behind: screen readers and audio description were rare. Studios’ own pages describe fewer than half of the applicable guidelines, and the award went to DOOM: The Dark Ages, third in our audit.

Figure 2 is a bar chart with two bars per game: the share of applicable guidelines implemented in play, and the share described on official pages. The games implement 74.5% to 84.2% of applicable guidelines in play, while their official pages describe 25.5% to 48.6%. The award winner, DOOM: The Dark Ages, is marked with a trophy.

Conceptual accessibility

Does the game, and its culture, make room for you?

A game can offer every setting and still push people away, through characters that reduce women to stereotypes or online spaces where players are harassed for who they are. Conceptual accessibility asks whether players can recognise themselves in a game and take part safely in its community.

Study 3. Stressing out the “damsel in distress”

Di Leo, Rizzi & Traetta, Postdigital Science and Education, 2025

For decades, many games cast women as the “damsel in distress”, who exists only to be rescued by the hero. We interviewed 19 Italian gamers (6 women, 13 men, aged 13 to 39) and read their answers through postdigital feminism, which treats online and offline life as one and asks how platforms reproduce social prejudice. We looked at three levels:

  • Representation. Participants see fewer hypersexualised women and more complex heroes like Samus Aran and Aloy, yet named mostly men as favourites.
  • Infrastructures. Voice chat that is on by default exposes women to harassment, so many mute the microphone or pick a male nickname.
  • Practices. Complaints about “forced representation” sat beside praise for subtle portrayals (Celeste) and calls for more diverse development teams.

“Boys pretend to be girls to receive positive attention from men, while girls pretend to be boys to avoid it.”

What connects the studies

Accessibility is negotiated across policy, technology and everyday practice (Ellcessor 2016). In these studies, a settings menu, a studio’s accessibility page and a voice-chat default all shape who gets to play. Checklists show which features are missing; reviews and interviews show who is left out.

Next step

Using these results to draft accessibility guidelines for games used in education.
